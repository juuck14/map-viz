Four colors,
one at a time

Every map can be colored with four colors so that no two neighbors match. That is the Four Color Theorem. It says the coloring exists. It does not say how to find it. Finding it is a search, and a search can be blind or it can look ahead. This page draws the search as it happens.

1

The rule

Two regions are neighbors when they share a stretch of border. Then they must get different colors. Regions that touch only at a single point () are not neighbors and may match.

Pick a color and click regions to paint. A border lights up red when both sides match.

2

Why three colors are not enough

Five regions around a center. You get only three colors here. Try it.

3

Every choice takes options away

An uncolored region stays bare while all four colors are open. Once a neighbor takes a color, it becomes striped with the colors it can still take. Paint a region and watch its color fly across each border: every neighbor loses that stripe.

A region down to one stripe has no choice left. A region with no stripes is dead: nothing you do to it can work, and some earlier choice must change. Try to color this map without killing a region.

4

Greedy: decide and never look back

The simplest method visits the regions in a fixed order and gives each one the first color still free (1 = blue, 2 = yellow, 3 = pink, 4 = green). It never revisits a choice.

Play it. Watch region L in the middle lose its stripes one by one. Greedy is not looking at L, so it walks straight into the trap.

5

Color the most cornered region first

The trap had a warning sign: a region with few stripes left. DSATUR (Brélaz, 1979) watches for it. It always colors next the region whose neighbors already use the most colors, which is the region with the fewest stripes. It deals with each emergency before it gets worse.

Same 32-region map, same rule for picking a color. Only the choice of the next region differs.

6

Backtracking: undo and try again

DSATUR usually works, but nothing guarantees it. Backtracking always finds a coloring if one exists. When a region has no color left, it goes back to the previous region in the order and tries that region's next color, then the one before, and so on.

Every attempt becomes a branch of a search tree: one level per region, one branch per color tried. Red branches were grown while some region on the map was already dead. Nothing grown there could ever succeed.

7

Most of the search was wasted

Plain backtracking only notices a dead region when it reaches that region in its order. Until then it keeps building on a coloring that cannot be completed: that is the red.

Forward checking keeps the stripes up to date for every region and undoes a choice the moment any region runs out. It never grows a red branch, so its tree is the same tree with every red branch cut off. Add DSATUR's idea, most cornered first, and there is almost nothing left to cut.

8

Kempe's trick: repair instead of undo

Back to greedy, stuck at L. Its five neighbors use all four colors. Instead of undoing anything, A. B. Kempe (1879) suggested swapping two colors in part of the map.

Pick a neighbor of L, say one colored yellow, and a second color, say green. Its Kempe chain is everything you can reach from it by walking through yellow and green regions. Swap yellow and green inside the chain. No new clash can appear: regions outside the chain that touch it are neither yellow nor green. If the chain doesn't come back to L through another neighbor, the swap frees a color for L.

Kempe believed this trick always works, and published it as a proof of the Four Color Theorem. Eleven years later, Heawood (1890) found a map where Kempe's double swap fails. The argument was still strong enough to prove that five colors always suffice. Four had to wait until 1976, when Appel and Haken checked 1,936 cases by computer.

9

Why a coloring always exists: peel the map

Euler's formula implies that every map has a region with at most five neighbors. Remove it. The rest is a smaller map, so it has one too. Keep peeling until nothing is left.

Then put the regions back in reverse order. Each one returns next to at most five colored neighbors, so with six colors a free one is always there. That is a complete proof that six colors suffice. Kempe chains bring it down to five.

How the page checks itself

Neighbors are computed from the drawn shapes: two borders must overlap along a segment. A separate checker uses the neighbor list the map generator recorded from its Voronoi cells, and every run shown here is re-checked step by step against it in the test suite. That includes the claim in section 7 that forward checking's tree is exactly backtracking's tree without the red branches.

The maps

Generated: Poisson-disc points become Voronoi cells, noise decides which cells are land, and regions grow from seeds at uneven speeds. Each shared border is stored once, so both neighbors use exactly the same line.
